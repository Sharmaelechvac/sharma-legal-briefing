Read the full story
  • What happened: The Florida Supreme Court (opinion by Justice Tanenbaum; Justice Labarga concurring in result) affirmed the Monroe County circuit court's summary denial of Thomas M. Overton's third successive postconviction motion, denied his habeas petition, and denied a stay of execution. Overton was sentenced to death in 1999 for the 1991 Florida Keys murders of Susan MacIvor — who was eight months pregnant — and Michael MacIvor; Gov. DeSantis signed the death warrant in October. The Court held: his public-records demands under Rule 3.852 were not relevant to a cognizable claim (no abuse of discretion in denying them — targeted evidence, not "a fishing expedition"); there is no freestanding "actual innocence" claim under Florida law independent of newly discovered evidence, and the claim was in any event untimely; his clemency-process due-process claim was legally non-cognizable; and, on the habeas challenge to Florida's capital scheme, the argument "has been raised with concerning frequency, despite this Court's repeated reminders that Florida's capital punishment process is wholly constitutional." Reporting on the decision says the Court issued its mandate immediately and barred rehearing requests, exhausting Overton's state-court options; his U.S. Supreme Court petition followed on Saturday (see §1.2). If carried out Thursday, it would be Florida's 20th execution this year, the most in 50 years, per CBS News as cited in the Nov 16 roundup.
  • Why it matters generally: Beyond the capital context, the decision is a clean statement of three Florida litigation rules that recur in civil cases too: records requests must tie to a cognizable claim, a claim that is not legally cognizable gets summarily denied without a hearing, and untimeliness is fatal even to sympathetic claims.
  • Sharma impact: No direct impact — Sharma has no criminal exposure and this changes no rule Sharma operates under. It is the most significant Florida court decision in the 72-hour window and is included for completeness of the Florida record; the transferable points are the deadline/records lessons above, which mirror the service-and-deadline lesson of Koppert in the Nov 15 briefing.

Sharma impact — No new employer duty today. The rule binds Florida's UC agency, not Sharma.

Read the full story
  • What happened: The U.S. Department of Labor's final rule on confidential unemployment compensation (UC) information (Employment and Training Administration, 91 FR 58593, published September 16, 2026) becomes effective today, Nov 16, 2026. The rule requires state UC agencies to disclose confidential UC information to federal officials for program oversight and audits. States whose laws need changing to comply have until no later than September 16, 2027. Contrary to some commentary, the final rule does not create a public national claims database — DOL says that proposal was discussed earlier but is not addressed in the final rule.
  • Why it matters generally: It is the one federal rule with a hard Nov 16 effective date anywhere in Sharma's compliance landscape, and it widens federal access to state unemployment-claims data — the same data employers contribute to through their separation responses.
  • Sharma impact: No new employer duty today. The rule binds Florida's UC agency, not Sharma. The indirect point stands: with federal auditors reading state UC files, the accuracy of Sharma's own UC paperwork (hire/separation dates, earnings, reason-for-separation responses) matters more, because inconsistencies between Sharma's records and the state file are exactly what oversight audits surface. Keep UC responses consistent with payroll records. (This rule's effective date was flagged in the Nov 15 briefing; it is carried today only because today is the effective date.)
