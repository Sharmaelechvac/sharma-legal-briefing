  • What happened: U.S. District Judge Jessica G. L. Clarke (S.D.N.Y.) on Monday, Nov 16, 2026 granted Anton and James Peraire-Bueno's motion for a judgment of acquittal on all counts — wire fraud, conspiracy to commit wire fraud, and conspiracy to commit money laundering — in United States v. Peraire-Bueno, No. 24-CR-293, and directed the clerk to close the case. The brothers, MIT graduates, were accused of taking about $25 million in about 12 seconds in April 2023 by exploiting a flaw in the MEV-Boost relay to front-run automated "sandwich" trading bots on Ethereum — the government's first criminal prosecution of a maximal-extractable-value (MEV) exploit. Judge Clarke's 46-page opinion holds the government never proved the element wire fraud cannot do without: a material misrepresentation — the brothers made no lie that a victim relied on; the relay never checked the falsified header fields, and no trader using the public mempool agreed to be sandwiched. Because the money-laundering conspiracy rested on the wire-fraud count, it fell with it. The ruling comes a year after the brothers' first trial ended in a mistrial on a deadlocked jury in November 2025; a judgment of acquittal ends the case with no retrial. The opinion expressly does not decide whether the conduct was ethical or violated uncharged laws. Dating stated plainly: the opinion is dated Monday, Nov 16; it was widely reported Nov 17–18 and was not in the Nov 17 or Nov 18 briefings, so it is counted here under the 72-hour widening with its true date shown.
  • Why it matters generally: It draws the current federal line for "code is law" conduct: exploiting a system's mechanics, without a false statement someone relied on, is not wire fraud on these facts. The holding is a district-court ruling — persuasive, not binding on other judges — but it is now the reference point for every crypto-exploit prosecution that follows.
  • Sharma impact: None direct — Sharma has no crypto-trading exposure. The transferable point is the fraud element itself, and it cuts in Sharma's favour as a potential victim: fraud claims (including the ones Sharma might bring over a doctored invoice or a false payment instruction) live or die on a provable misrepresentation that was relied on — which is why Sharma's paper trail (signed proposals, written change orders, verified payment instructions) is the asset that matters if a payment is ever diverted.

Read the full story
  • What happened: A panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit — Judges Elizabeth Branch, Andrew Brasher and Frank Hull, in a 16-page published opinion by Judge Brasher — on Tuesday, Nov 17, 2026 denied the petition for review in Aguiar-Fernandez v. U.S. Attorney General, No. 24-12318, holding that release at the border on Form I-220A (an Order of Release on Recognizance under 8 U.S.C. § 1226) is not the "parole" the Cuban Adjustment Act (CAA) requires. The court read the CAA's "paroled into the United States" to mean humanitarian parole under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(d)(5)(A), adopting the Second Circuit's rule in Cruz-Miguel v. Holder and aligning with the Third and Ninth Circuits and the BIA's Matter of Castillo-Padilla; § 1226's "conditional parole" is a different mechanism that "does not suffice." Whether DHS granted humanitarian parole is "a question of historical fact" turning on the reason the agency actually gave — here the record was clear (I-220As citing § 1226, no Form I-94, and DHS had denied the family's humanitarian-parole requests), so the family — who entered near San Luis, Arizona on June 25, 2021 — could not adjust status under the CAA; the panel also upheld the denials of asylum, withholding and CAT protection under the substantial-evidence standard. It is the first published Eleventh Circuit decision on the question and is binding precedent in Florida, Georgia and Alabama. It does not decide the pending Southern District of Florida suit by roughly 992 I-220A holders, Bello-Rubio v. Noem, No. 1:25-cv-23665. A rehearing petition would generally be due about Jan 1, 2027. Dating stated plainly: the opinion is dated Tuesday, Nov 17; the Nov 18 briefing's appellate sweep did not locate it, and it is counted here under the 72-hour widening with its true date shown.
  • Why it matters generally: Tens of thousands of Cubans released since 2021 hold I-220As instead of parole, and a large share live in Florida. A published circuit holding — rather than agency practice or an unpublished order — now fixes their CAA eligibility in the circuit that hears most of these cases: an I-220A alone is not enough.
  • Sharma impact: No direct exposure — this is an immigration-status ruling about a specific adjustment path, not an employment-authorization rule, and it changes nothing about I-9 or E-Verify duties, which turn on the work-authorization documents an employee presents, not on this case. It is carried because it is the week's most consequential binding Florida-circuit precedent and because it will be widely discussed in Florida's Cuban community, including among construction workers and their families. Sharma should not attempt to apply it to any individual — status questions belong to immigration counsel.

Read the full story
  • What happened: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit on Wednesday, Nov 18, 2026 held in Laurenzano v. Commissioner, No. 25-2020 that the 90-day deadline in 26 U.S.C. § 6213(a) for petitioning the U.S. Tax Court for redetermination of a deficiency is a nonjurisdictional claims-processing rule, not a limit on the Tax Court's power — so the Tax Court should not have dismissed Peter Laurenzano's late-filed petition (challenging tax penalties) without considering extenuating circumstances. The panel vacated the dismissal and remanded for the Tax Court to decide whether equitable tolling applies on Laurenzano's facts. The Fourth Circuit thereby joined a widening circuit split post-Boechler, P.C. v. Commissioner, 596 U.S. 199 (2022): the Second, Third, Sixth and Eighth Circuits have held the deadline nonjurisdictional and tollable; the First Circuit has held it nonjurisdictional but a mandatory, non-tollable claims-processing rule. Critically for Florida readers: the same question is pending and undecided in the Eleventh Circuit in Reddick v. Commissioner, No. 26-10999 — so Wednesday's ruling is persuasive, not binding, here. Congress has also taken note: the Senate-passed amended Taxpayer Assistance and Service (TAS) Act (H.R. 5349, passed Sep 30, 2026) would resolve the split legislatively by making the § 6213(a) deadline nonjurisdictional and tollable; it awaits House action on the amended version.
  • Why it matters generally: For decades the 90-day rule was treated as absolute — miss it and the Tax Court could not hear the case at all, leaving pay-first refund litigation as the only route. The post-Boechler clear-statement framework has now moved five circuits off that position, and a late petition backed by genuine extenuating circumstances (illness, bad advice, a notice gone astray) is increasingly a tolling argument rather than an automatic loss.
  • Sharma impact: Directly relevant to Sharma's tax hygiene, with the limit stated plainly. (1) Do not rely on this ruling: Florida sits in the Eleventh Circuit, where Reddick is still pending — today, a late § 6213(a) petition from a Florida taxpayer is still at serious risk of dismissal, and Sharma's rule remains unchanged: a notice of deficiency starts a hard 90-day clock (150 days if addressed abroad) and goes to the CPA/tax counsel the day it arrives. (2) If Sharma or an owner ever has missed such a deadline, Wednesday's decision plus the four circuits behind it are the argument to put in counsel's hands immediately — and the Senate-passed TAS Act language shows where Congress is heading. (3) Nothing about ordinary filing deadlines (returns, estimates, information returns — see the FIRE reminder at §3.3) is changed by this case.

  • What happened: The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit on Wednesday, Nov 18, 2026 affirmed dismissal of an antitrust lawsuit by California cardholders accusing Visa, Mastercard, JPMorgan Chase, Citibank and other major banks of conspiring to fix debit and credit card interchange fees, holding the cardholders failed to plead standing because they did not allege a cognizable antitrust injury — as consumers, they are too far removed from the interchange transactions, which are set by the networks and charged to acquiring banks that process payments.   • What happened: A federal judge in the Southern District of New York ruled on Tuesday, Nov 17, 2026 in JPMorgan Chase & Co. v. City of Richmond, No. 1:26-cv-01143-VM, dismissing JPMorgan's complaint seeking to enforce a 2015 settlement of municipal-derivatives antitrust claims (bid-rigging, price-fixing, market manipulation) as a bar to the City of Richmond, California's separate California False Claims Act suit over interest-rate swaps tied to its federally taxable pension obligation bonds. The court held the 2015 release covered derivatives associated with tax-exempt debt and claims that were or could have been alleged in the antitrust MDL — it did not encompass Richmond's swaps (federally taxable) or its unrelated fraudulent-inducement and false-certification allegations against JPMorgan's predecessor, Bear Stearns, from 2007 swaps. Richmond's FCA action therefore proceeds, unimpeded by the settlement. Dating stated plainly: the decision is dated Nov 17 in the case caption reporting; it was reported Nov 18 and was not in any prior briefing.
  • Why it matters generally: It is a clean lesson in release drafting: a settlement releases what its text covers — here, a defined category of transactions — and a party that drafts (or signs) a release tied to one category cannot later stretch it over a different category of deals or a different kind of wrongdoing.
  • Sharma impact: No exposure — Sharma is not a party and holds no swaps. The transferable point is contractual and general: releases and settlement language in Sharma's own world (change-order settlements, final-payment releases, lien waivers) will be read by their defined scope, not by what one side later wishes they covered — which is why the scope language in any release Sharma signs deserves the same care as the price.
