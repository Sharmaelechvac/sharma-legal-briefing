  • What happened: On Friday, Nov 20, Judge Timothy Boyer of the St. Louis, Missouri circuit court gave final approval to Bayer's nationwide settlement of the Roundup weed-killer litigation, a deal that could cost the company up to $7.25 billion in the coming years. The money goes to thousands of people who have been or will be diagnosed with non-Hodgkin lymphoma that they attribute to glyphosate, Roundup's main ingredient; under the settlement, affected people could receive $10,000 to $165,000 depending on occupational exposure, age at diagnosis, and other factors. Judge Boyer called it "among the largest in the history of mass tort personal-injury class action settlements." Plaintiffs' attorney Christopher Seeger said the final approval order "delivers guaranteed compensation to Roundup cancer victims who have waited more than a decade for justice," and Bayer CEO Bill Anderson called the settlement a "decisive milestone" that will "mitigate the risks" of pending and future litigation. The approval comes five months after the U.S. Supreme Court ruled for Bayer in the Durnell case (a 7–2 decision holding that federal pesticide law barred the state-law failure-to-warn claim), which narrowed the path for individual suits and pushed the parties toward this class resolution. Bayer faced roughly 65,000 claims in U.S. state and federal courts; this settlement is on top of the roughly $10–$11 billion Bayer paid in 2020 to settle more than 100,000 earlier claims. Bayer continues to deny glyphosate causes cancer, and the EPA has concluded it did not identify cancer risks of concern from glyphosate uses, though an EPA review is ongoing.
  • Why it matters: It is the closing chapter — subject to any appeal of the approval — of the largest product-liability saga of the decade, and a template for how a Supreme Court preemption win converts into a capped, class-wide payout rather than open-ended jury exposure.
  • Sharma impact: No direct duty for Sharma: Sharma does not manufacture or sell herbicides. The transferable lessons are the ones this briefing keeps drawing from mass-tort news — a company's warnings, labels, and safety documentation are its litigation record years later, and a single adverse ruling theory (here, failure to warn) can aggregate into billions. Sharma's equivalent exposure is jobsite chemical and equipment documentation: SDS files, HazCom training records (the substances deadline passed Friday, §3.4), and equipment manuals left with customers.

2.1 — LATE-LOCATED (filed Friday, Nov 20; Attorney General's release dated Nov 20; not in yesterday's edition) — AG Uthmeier sues The Leapfrog Group and its CEO under FDUTPA over hospital safety grades — Friday, Nov 20, 2026.

Attorney General James Uthmeier filed suit in the Circuit Court of the Eighteenth Judicial Circuit (Seminole County) against The Leapfrog Group and its President and CEO, Leah Binder, alleging violations of the Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act (FDUTPA) and Florida's Solicitation of Contributions Act.

Sharma impact — The direct lesson is about published claims: FDUTPA liability does not require a product sale — marketing representations ("peer-reviewed," "expert-guided") that the underlying method doesn't support are enough.

Read the full story
  • What happened: Attorney General James Uthmeier filed suit in the Circuit Court of the Eighteenth Judicial Circuit (Seminole County) against The Leapfrog Group and its President and CEO, Leah Binder, alleging violations of the Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act (FDUTPA) and Florida's Solicitation of Contributions Act. Leapfrog publishes letter safety grades for roughly 190 Florida hospitals twice a year and markets its Hospital Safety Grade as "the first tool patients should use to select a hospital." The complaint alleges that, beginning with the Fall 2024 grades, hospitals that declined Leapfrog's voluntary survey were assigned the lowest score any hospital had received on four safety measures — on measures for which Leapfrog had no data at all for those hospitals — while the weight of those measures rose from 24% to 31% of the overall grade; that Leapfrog told consumers the grades were peer-reviewed and expert-guided without disclosing the practice; and that the change coincided with a projected revenue shortfall of about $540,000, while Leapfrog's largest revenue source is "accolade" licenses sold to hospitals for $6,700–$29,900 a year to advertise their grades. One Florida hospital that had scored 100 of 100 on hand hygiene was later scored 15 under the new method, the complaint says. The State seeks a permanent bar on misrepresenting the data, sources, methods, or expert approvals behind any grade published to Florida consumers, corrective disclosures, restitution and disgorgement, and civil penalties of up to $10,000 per willful violation — up to $15,000 per violation involving a senior citizen — plus fees and costs. Uthmeier also filed an amicus brief in the Eleventh Circuit in the related private case brought by five Palm Beach County hospitals (in which a federal judge ordered those hospitals' grades removed; Leapfrog is appealing), arguing the First Amendment does not immunize deceptive conduct. Leapfrog responded that the suit is "naked threats of government censorship" piggybacking on the hospitals' case and that it will continue publishing.
  • Why it matters: It is a sitting attorney general using FDUTPA — Florida's broad consumer-protection statute, the same one that reaches deceptive trade practices by any Florida business — against a ratings publisher, on the theory that a score presented as data-driven but built partly on no data is a deceptive practice. The First Amendment defense will make it a closely watched case on where rating and review speech ends and actionable deception begins.
  • Sharma impact: The direct lesson is about published claims: FDUTPA liability does not require a product sale — marketing representations ("peer-reviewed," "expert-guided") that the underlying method doesn't support are enough. Sharma's own published claims — reviews solicited, ratings displayed, "licensed and insured" statements, SEER/efficiency figures quoted to homeowners — should be exactly as supportable as the Leapfrog grades were alleged not to be. No action is required; this is a standards reminder, not a Sharma matter.

  • What happened: A Multnomah County (Oregon) Circuit Court jury on Friday, Nov 20 found for former Adidas manager April Burton on one of her five claims — that Adidas subjected her to a hostile work environment because of her race — and awarded $1.23 million, all of it punitive damages, with no compensatory damages (Law360's report). The jury found for Adidas on the other four claims, including gender discrimination, whistleblower retaliation, and wage claims, and did not find Adidas took an adverse employment action against Burton because of her race. Burton, who managed Beyoncé's Ivy Park product line from Adidas's Portland office, had sought $6.155 million in economic and emotional damages plus $10 million in punitive damages; her salary was $121,680, according to her lawyer. The case is April Burton v. Adidas America Inc., No. 24CV43621.
  • Why it matters: A split verdict with a punitive-only award is the pattern employers underestimate: even where the jury rejects the firing/retaliation theory, a documented pattern of racially charged remarks left unaddressed can carry a seven-figure punitive price on the hostile-environment claim alone.
  • Sharma impact: A training-grade example for a field workforce: complaints about slurs or hazing on a crew need a documented response when they are made, not after a termination decision gives them a litigation frame. Sharma's exposure is the same shape — small company, close crews, and a paper trail that either exists or doesn't.

  • What happened: A federal jury in Omaha, Nebraska (the trial venue in the Courthouse News reporting) on Friday, Nov 20 found TIG Insurance Company liable for breach of its reinsurance contract with National Indemnity Insurance Company, awarding $39.2 million in indemnity plus $40.4 million in interest and expenses ($3.2 million prejudgment interest, $21.1 million postjudgment interest, and $16.1 million in expenses), while declining to award future defense costs. National Indemnity insured the State of Montana from July 1973 to July 1975 and settled all coverage claims over asbestos contamination from the Libby Mine vermiculite operation for $157 million in 2022; it billed its reinsurers more than $101 million, and TIG paid $12.7 million and disputed the rest, arguing National Indemnity billed amounts not covered and inflated the occurrence count. The jury found National Indemnity had fulfilled its obligations under the reinsurance certificate. This item is carried at listing level: the verdict detail located comes from aggregator summaries of Courthouse News' trial coverage, not from a primary wire report read directly.
  • Why it matters: Long-tail liability is the point: insurance written in 1973–1975 produced a nine-figure dispute resolved in 2026. Reinsurance and excess carriers pay on documentation — occurrence counts, notice, and claims handling — decades after the work.
  • Sharma impact: Sharma's version is smaller but structurally identical: completed-operations coverage for electrical and HVAC work responds years after trim-out, and whether it responds turns on the paper — certificates, notice, and records kept from the job. Keep certificates of insurance and job files for the long tail, not the tax year.

Marion Superior Court Judge Patrick Dietrick ruled Wednesday, Nov 18 dismissing Attorney General Todd Rokita's lawsuit alleging the Aylo conglomerate's websites violated Indiana's 2024 age-verification law.

  • What happened: Marion Superior Court Judge Patrick Dietrick ruled Wednesday, Nov 18 dismissing Attorney General Todd Rokita's lawsuit alleging the Aylo conglomerate's websites violated Indiana's 2024 age-verification law. The State's evidence showed an investigator accessing 42 challenged websites through a VPN with a Chicago IP address; the court held the suit documented no instance of access from an Indiana IP address, declined to read an "actual-location rule" or a duty to defeat VPN circumvention into the statute, and held that recognising Indiana users can access a site does not prove a violation. The Attorney General's office did not comment in the Nov 21 report; it may appeal or refile with new evidence.
  • Why it matters: A consumer-protection enforcement lesson in evidence geography: a state statute reaches conduct the State can actually place inside the state, and VPN-masked test purchases don't place it there.
  • Sharma impact: Minimal and indirect — noted because Sharma's own compliance world (E-Verify, online ads, website claims) runs on the same principle: what matters is the documented, located record, not the assertion.
