Read the full story
  • What happened: The Justice Department told a federal court in a filing late on Saturday, Nov 21 that the U.S. Army will carry out a controlled detonation of 68 barrels of degraded lead azide — a toxic explosive used in military munitions — near Middletown, Iowa, by Tuesday, November 24, 2026, invoking an emergency munitions and explosives exception to federal environmental protection laws so it can proceed without a permit. "The Army must perform a controlled detonation to protect the public from the more significant threat from an uncontrolled explosion, and the Army intends to act by November 24, 2026," the filing said. Federal officials say falling temperatures could destabilise the material and cause an uncontrolled explosion within days. The move bypasses the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, which on Thursday, Nov 19 temporarily halted the detonation by freezing the Army's environmental permit after the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club's Iowa chapter (represented by Earthjustice) sued; the plaintiffs argue the detonation would release a toxic plume of lead dust that could travel 100 miles. The Army had previously postponed the detonation after public outcry by residents and farmers near Middletown, and acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle said Thursday the Army and the EPA had determined a controlled detonation posed the lowest risk to the public. The Army did not respond on Sunday to a question about the precise timing.
  • Why it matters: An executive agency announcing in a court filing that it will act without the permit a federal appeals court has frozen — on an emergency exception — is the weekend's most consequential legal event: it tests how far emergency exceptions to environmental law reach against a live court order, and whatever the court does next (the plaintiffs can be expected to move immediately) will be the precedent.
  • Sharma impact: No direct duty — the site is in Iowa and Sharma does no federal munitions work. The transferable point is the one Sharma's own world runs on: permit freezes and stop-work postures are normally absolute until lifted, and the exception being claimed here is a narrow, safety-of-life emergency carve-out — not a model for working around a permit hold on ordinary construction work. Noted also because lead exposure is a HazCom/OSHA subject Sharma's crews already track (§3.1).

1.2 — LATE-LOCATED (ruling Friday, Nov 20; reported Sunday, Nov 22; not in the Nov 21 edition) — Federal judge blocks Connecticut from enforcing its ICE mask ban and use-of-force rules against federal agents — Friday, Nov 20, 2026.

U.S. District Judge Vernon D. Oliver ruled Friday, Nov 20 granting the Trump administration's request for a preliminary injunction against four sections of Connecticut's Senate Bill 397, the state's new law regulating federal immigration agents.

Sharma impact — None direct — Sharma has no Connecticut or federal-agent exposure. Carried as the week's significant federalism ruling and background to the state-vs-federal enforcement climate.

Read the full story
  • What happened: U.S. District Judge Vernon D. Oliver ruled Friday, Nov 20 granting the Trump administration's request for a preliminary injunction against four sections of Connecticut's Senate Bill 397, the state's new law regulating federal immigration agents. The 39-page ruling holds the provisions likely violate the Supremacy Clause: "SB 397 seeks to regulate federal officers' conduct. But federal law already governs federal officers' conduct." The blocked sections would have barred officers including federal agents from wearing face coverings while dealing with the public (with health, safety, and undercover exceptions), required a badge and name tag during planned arrests, applied Connecticut's deadly/physical-force standards to federal officers, and let the state inspector general investigate deaths involving federal officers and prosecute where force was found unjustified; breaking the mask or identification rules would have been a class D misdemeanour. Oliver wrote the mask and identification rules would put federal officers and their families at "a heightened risk of retributive attacks." He dismissed Attorney General William Tong from the case as not a proper defendant and rejected the state's standing argument. Governor Lamont said he was "disappointed" and condemned ICE's treatment of Connecticut residents; Tong's office is reviewing next steps. The report notes a federal judge in California earlier in the week permanently blocked two California laws that would have required immigration agents to remove their masks.
  • Why it matters: The second federal ruling in a week holding that states may not regulate how federal officers dress, identify themselves, or use force — the Supremacy Clause line between state police power and federal operations is being drawn in the immigration context first, but the reasoning is general.
  • Sharma impact: None direct — Sharma has no Connecticut or federal-agent exposure. Carried as the week's significant federalism ruling and background to the state-vs-federal enforcement climate.

Sharma impact — None direct. Background First Amendment law only; a filing, not a ruling — the Court has not agreed to hear the case.

Read the full story
  • What happened: The ACLU announced Friday, Nov 20 that it had filed a petition for certiorari on behalf of DeRay Mckesson in Mckesson v. Ford, asking the Supreme Court: "Does the First Amendment foreclose a state law negligence action making a leader of a political protest liable in damages for injuries caused by an unidentified third party's violent act, absent evidence that the defendant directed, ratified, authorized, or intended the assault, or violence of any kind?" The case grows out of a July 9, 2016 Baton Rouge protest over the police killing of Alton Sterling, at which an unidentified person threw a rock or piece of concrete that seriously injured officer John Ford; Mckesson did not throw it. A district court granted Mckesson summary judgment in 2024; the Fifth Circuit reversed, 2–1, on March 19, 2026, holding he could be liable in negligence for failing to use reasonable care. The Supreme Court previously declined to hear the case in April 2024, with Justice Sotomayor writing that Counterman v. Colorado (2023) — negligence is not the standard even for true threats — should govern future proceedings. The petition was filed a week ahead of a November 27 deadline Justice Alito set; as of Sunday, Nov 22, the Supreme Court's docket showed no case number yet for the petition.
  • Why it matters: If the Court takes the case, it will define the civil-liability exposure of anyone who organises a protest in the Fifth Circuit's negligence framework — a First Amendment question the Court has circled three times in this same litigation since 2020.
  • Sharma impact: None direct. Background First Amendment law only; a filing, not a ruling — the Court has not agreed to hear the case.
