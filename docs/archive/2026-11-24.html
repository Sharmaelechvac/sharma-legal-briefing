  • What happened: In an unsigned order on Monday, Nov 23, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit denied the emergency stay motion brought by the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club's Iowa chapter and lifted the administrative stay it had imposed on Thursday, Nov 19 on the EPA permit authorising the controlled detonation of 68 barrels (nearly 9,500 pounds) of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown. The court held the groups "failed to show they are likely to prevail," concluding: "We conclude that the EPA adequately incorporated safeguards to minimize the dispersal of lead dust into the environment and mitigate the risks associated with potential exposure pathways." It also found the Army had evaluated "a full range" of alternatives — on-site chemical treatment, closed-chamber thermal treatment, relocating the drums, and detonation inside the earth-covered magazines — and that the EPA reasonably relied on the Army's assessment; and it weighed the detonation's harm against "the realities of the situation: the degraded explosives present a risk of an uncontrolled and unplanned explosion." Earlier Monday, President Trump posted on Truth Social that he had ordered the military to halt the plan until the Eighth Circuit agreed it could go forward and Governor-elect Rob Sand agreed to the operation, saying he would hold Sand accountable if the plan did not proceed and the material exploded spontaneously. Sand responded Monday afternoon that the administration "should make this decision based on the information they have from the people they brought in to do the job," and asked that briefing information be shared with the public. The plaintiffs' counsel, Earthjustice managing attorney Rachel Rintelmann, said Monday night: "Today, the Eighth Circuit decided to let the Army proceed with its plan, and we respect that decision," while adding that "the Army created this emergency through neglect." In a statement late Monday, the Army said it is proceeding with the controlled removal in coordination with state, local and federal authorities, and acting Secretary Adam Telle wrote that the detonation will happen Wednesday morning "as previously scheduled, based on forecast wind conditions" — Wednesday is November 25, 2026. The Army's Saturday-night position that it could proceed without the permit under an emergency exception is now moot: it will act under the permit the court has un-frozen.
  • Why it matters: The legal fight is over in the only court that had stopped the blast: a federal appeals court has held, on a likelihood-of-success standard, that the EPA's safeguards and alternatives analysis were adequate — and the President's same-day pause order was satisfied on its own terms as to the court within hours. What remains is execution and monitoring, on the permit's own conditions.
  • Sharma impact: No direct duty — Iowa, federal munitions, no Sharma work. The transferable point stands from yesterday, now with the ending written: the permit freeze was lifted by the court that imposed it, through the stay process — the ordinary rule (a hold is a hold until the issuing authority lifts it) is exactly how this resolved.

3.1 — UPDATE — The DOL independent-contractor rule is PUBLISHED: Federal Register, Monday, Nov 23, 2026 (91 FR 75646); effective Friday, January 22, 2027 (resolving the Nov 21–23 editions' watch item).

The U.S. Department of Labor's final rule, "Employee or Independent Contractor Status Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, Family and Medical Leave Act, and Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Worker Protection Act" (Federal Register Document 2026-23777; Docket No. WHD-2026-0001; RIN 1235-AA46),

Sharma impact — Direct and actionable. Sharma's use of any subcontracted or 1099 labour is judged under this test from Jan 22, 2027: the two questions that will matter most are how much control Sharma exercises over the worker's schedule, methods, and other work, and whether the worker genuinely stands to profit or lose on the job.

Read the full story
  • What happened: The U.S. Department of Labor's final rule, "Employee or Independent Contractor Status Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, Family and Medical Leave Act, and Migrant and Seasonal Agricultural Worker Protection Act" (Federal Register Document 2026-23777; Docket No. WHD-2026-0001; RIN 1235-AA46), was published in the Federal Register on Monday, November 23, 2026, at 91 FR 75646, and takes effect 60 days later, on Friday, January 22, 2027. As announced Nov 19 and carried in prior editions, the rule rescinds the 2024 rule and restores an "economic reality" test similar to the 2021 rule, codified at 29 C.F.R. Part 795: five non-exhaustive factors, with two core factors carrying the most weight — (1) the nature and degree of control over the work and (2) the worker's opportunity for profit or loss based on initiative or investment — plus the amount of skill required, the permanence of the working relationship, and whether the work is part of an integrated unit of production. No single factor is determinative. The same standard now applies under the FLSA, the FMLA, and the MSPA. DOL considered more than 16,000 public comments. Employer-side analyses published Monday note the rule generally makes contractor classification easier to sustain than under the 2024 six-factor test, that stricter state law still governs where it applies, and that court challenges are likely; the New Civil Liberties Alliance, which sued over the 2024 rule in Colt & Joe Trucking v. DOL, claimed the rescission as a victory in a Nov 23 statement. No lawsuit challenging the 2026 rule had appeared in the sources reviewed as of Nov 23.
  • Why it matters: Publication is the legally operative step — it fixes the effective date and starts the countdown. From January 22, 2027, this control + profit/loss test is the federal standard DOL will apply in investigations; classification files built under the 2024 test should be re-papered against it before then.
  • Sharma impact: Direct and actionable. Sharma's use of any subcontracted or 1099 labour is judged under this test from Jan 22, 2027: the two questions that will matter most are how much control Sharma exercises over the worker's schedule, methods, and other work, and whether the worker genuinely stands to profit or lose on the job. The rule does not displace Florida law or the IRS's separate test, and it does not make any current arrangement automatically compliant — it changes the federal measuring stick, not the facts. File the citation: 91 FR 75646 (Nov 23, 2026).
