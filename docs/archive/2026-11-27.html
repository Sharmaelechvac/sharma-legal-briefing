1.1 UPDATE — Second Circuit revives the bid to move the New York hush-money case to federal court — Mon Nov 23, 2026 [72-hr widen]; AP wire confirmation located Thu Nov 26

  • What happened: On Monday, November 23, 2026, a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit — Circuit Judges Raymond J. Lohier Jr., José A. Cabranes and John M. Walker Jr. — issued a two-page order reversing the August 2026 decision of the late U.S. District Judge Alvin Hellerstein (who died in October) that had denied, for a second time, the President's request to remove his New York state criminal case to federal court. The panel held Hellerstein erred in two respects: (i) in holding that removal is categorically unavailable in a state criminal case after the defendant has been convicted, and (ii) in denying that the President had shown "good cause" to file a second removal notice — the changed legal landscape after the Supreme Court's July 2024 presidential-immunity decision (Trump v. United States, 603 U.S. 593) supplied that cause. The panel ordered the district court — the case is now before U.S. District Judge Lewis J. Liman, who inherited it on Hellerstein's death — to promptly decide whether the Manhattan federal court should retain the case, said a full written opinion will follow "in due course," and its ruling set in motion a halt to proceedings in the parallel state-court appeal (argued before the Appellate Division on November 5). The President was convicted in May 2024 on 34 felony counts of falsifying business records to conceal a $130,000 payment to Stormy Daniels and received an unconditional discharge in January 2025. Status of yesterday's item: the November 26 edition carried this ruling as UNVERIFIED because only partisan secondary sources (a Conservative Institute item dated November 26, citing Breitbart and Just the News) had been located. The Associated Press report of the ruling is dated November 23, 2026 — the ruling was never a November 26 event; the partisan item simply re-dated it. It is now confirmed and is carried here at its true date.
  • Why it matters: Removal doctrine is the sleeper issue: if a federal-officer defendant can remove after conviction on the strength of an intervening Supreme Court immunity ruling, every state prosecution touching federal duties gets a second forum fight after verdict. The panel decided only forum and timing — not immunity, and not the merits. If Judge Liman keeps the case, the federal court adopts the conviction and the fight becomes whether it must be vacated under the immunity standard; a presidential pardon could not reach the underlying New York state offences either way.
  • Effect on Sharma: None directly. The transferable point is procedural and worth Harvey noting for any Sharma matter: an intervening appellate decision can reopen a forum or deadline question that looked closed — "we already lost that motion" is not the end of the analysis when the governing law changes mid-case.

1.2 Virginia Supreme Court: a felony conviction alone does not strip Second Amendment rights — Ginevan v. Commonwealth, Wed Nov 25, 2026 (late-located; reported in full Thu Nov 26)

  • What happened: On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Supreme Court of Virginia, in a unanimous opinion by Justice Wesley G. Russell Jr., held in Ginevan v. Commonwealth, Record No. 250051, that Virginia's felon-in-possession statute, Code § 18.2-308.2, is unconstitutional as applied to Derek Wade Ginevan, whose record contained no finding of violence. The court held, first, that "a felony conviction alone does not remove one from 'the people' protected by the Second Amendment" — the same answer, it noted, every federal circuit to decide the question has given — and, second, applying the Bruen text-and-history framework, that the Commonwealth failed to carry its burden of showing that permanently disarming Ginevan fits the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation: at argument the State could not identify a single American statute banning all convicted felons from possessing firearms before the federal efforts of the 1960s. The court reversed the Court of Appeals, vacated the conviction and entered final judgment for Ginevan. The court expressly characterised its decision as narrow: a different offence, criminal history or evidentiary record "may well lead to a different conclusion," and it left open whether a permanent — as opposed to temporary — ban can ever be justified.
  • Why it matters: This is a state high court working the Bruen framework to an as-applied invalidation of a blanket felon ban, one week before the U.S. Supreme Court's December 2 argument in the consolidated Cook County AR-15 cases (Khaya, No. 25-238). It does not facially invalidate § 18.2-308.2, which remains in force for everyone else, and it does not touch the separate federal felon-in-possession ban, 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Expect case-by-case as-applied challenges turning on each defendant's record.
  • Effect on Sharma: None directly — Virginia law, and the federal ban still applies regardless. Noted because Second Amendment scope is a live December item at the U.S. Supreme Court (see the deadlines table) and because background-check and workplace-policy questions occasionally surface around hires with old convictions: this ruling changes nothing in Florida and nothing federally.

  • What happened: On Monday, November 23, 2026, U.S. District Judge Margaret I. Strickland (District of New Mexico) issued a 29-page order in Mescalero Apache Tribe v. 2.1 UPDATE — Florida Supreme Court disbars Natalie Patricia Whitco — The Florida Bar v. Whitco, No. SC2024-1870, Wed Nov 25, 2026 (opinion text now available; resolves the November 25–26 editions' UNVERIFIED item)

  • What happened: In a per curiam opinion dated Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Supreme Court of Florida disbarred Natalie Patricia Whitco from the practice of law in Florida, effective immediately (she was already suspended), with judgment for The Florida Bar for $2,097.00 in costs. Whitco was admitted in October 2004 and suspended for three years on February 14, 2019 after a conviction for resisting an officer with violence; her reinstatement petition was later dismissed after she misrepresented herself as a licensed attorney at a public hearing while suspended. The referee recommended findings of guilt and permanent disbarment. The Court approved the guilt findings under Rule 3-4.3 (an act unlawful or contrary to honesty and justice) and Rule 4-8.4(b) (a criminal act reflecting adversely on honesty, trustworthiness or fitness) — the record includes the November 18, 2022 incident in which Whitco turned her car toward a bystander who had told her to slow down, jumped the curb, approached him aggressively, then fled from officers, kicked one in the abdomen and had to be tased repeatedly, pleading guilty on March 13, 2023 to resisting without violence and reckless driving — and a May 2021 arrest for aggravated battery with a deadly weapon and domestic battery by strangulation (not prosecuted after the spouse declined to cooperate). The Court disapproved the guilt recommendation under Rule 3-7.2(c) (the 10-day felony-charge notification rule): no information or indictment was ever filed in the 2021 matter, and the record did not establish when felony charges were filed in the 2022 matter, so the notification duty was never shown to have been triggered. On sanction, the Court held disbarment — not permanent disbarment — was the right incremental step: most of Whitco's many arrests ended in diversion or withheld adjudication and only one produced a conviction, so she had not been shown to be beyond rehabilitation. Justice Labarga dissented, arguing permanent disbarment was warranted. Status of the prior item: the November 25 edition carried this disbarment as UNVERIFIED (a paywalled Law.com report that did not name the attorney in its accessible portion), and the November 26 edition carried it forward as still unconfirmed. The opinion text is now available and confirms the report in substance, including the "disbarred but not permanently disbarred" detail.
  • Why it matters: Two durable points. First, the sanction ladder: even a long arrest pattern produced the lesser sanction where convictions were few — Florida discipline turns on proven conduct, not arrest counts. Second, the Rule 3-7.2(c) ruling is a clean evidence lesson: a notification duty that depends on a filing date cannot be proven without putting the charging document in the record.
  • Effect on Sharma: No direct effect — Sharma is not a law firm. The general lesson transfers to any licensed trade: in DBPR/ECLB discipline, as in Bar discipline, the sanction follows the proven violations and the licensee's prior proven record, and charges the agency cannot document get dismissed. Keep Sharma's own compliance record document-clean for the same reason.

2.2 First DCA reverses the fraud findings in a $34.7 million tobacco verdict and orders a new damages trial — R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Bessent-Dixon, Wed Nov 25, 2026 (late-located; reported in full Thu Nov 26)

  • What happened: On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Florida First District Court of Appeal (Judges Kelsey, M.K. Thomas and Long, unanimous) decided R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Company v. Bessent-Dixon, Estate of Tyrone M. Dixon, No. 1D2024-1920, out of Alachua County. Frances Bessent-Dixon, suing individually and as personal representative of her late husband Tyrone M. Dixon, had won a jury verdict finding Reynolds 60% at fault and awarding $8,975,000 in compensatory damages and $25,748,176 in punitive damages — $34,723,176 in total. The panel held the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law to establish detrimental reliance — Florida fraudulent-concealment law in these Engle-progeny tobacco cases requires proof the smoker relied on a deceptive statement and was harmed by it, and liability on this record would have required the jury to stack assumptions rather than rely on proof. It directed the trial court to enter judgment for Reynolds on the fraudulent-concealment and conspiracy-to-commit-fraudulent-concealment claims. Because the jury had weighed the fraud claims together with the surviving strict-liability and negligence claims in fixing fault and damages, the compensatory and punitive awards could not be severed and were set aside, with a new trial on compensatory and punitive damages ordered on the remaining claims. The panel also held the trial court had not performed the required legal analysis weighing Reynolds' First Amendment commercial-speech rights before admitting evidence of race-targeted marketing, and remanded that evidence question for the proper analysis — it did not hold the evidence can never be admitted. The case has already been through a 2018 mistrial and a January 2021 First DCA reversal for a jury-instruction error on reliance.
  • Why it matters: Reliance is an element, not a presumption: a fraud verdict built on a jury inferring that a consumer must have believed industry statements will not survive appeal without record proof connecting a specific statement to the consumer's beliefs and choices. The same element discipline applies to FDUTPA and common-law fraud claims against businesses generally — including contractors (compare §4.1, where the fraud claim survived the merits fight only to be sent to arbitration).
  • Effect on Sharma: No direct effect (tobacco). The transferable point is about Sharma's own consumer-facing statements: fraud exposure turns on what a specific customer was told and relied on — which is why written scopes, written exclusions and signed proposals are the defence file. Verbal promises by sales or field staff are the reliance evidence in the other side's case.

3.1 Insurance: a mold endorsement does not stack on top of the water-damage cap — People's Trust Insurance Co. v. Molina, Third DCA, Wed Nov 25, 2026 (late-located; reported Thu Nov 26)

  • What happened: On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Florida Third District Court of Appeal decided People's Trust Insurance Company v. Jacinto Molina, reversing a trial-court award of an additional $10,000 in mold benefits on top of the $10,000 the insurer had already paid. The policyholder's Limited Water Damage Coverage endorsement covered water discharge from an air-conditioning system, capped at $10,000; the policy also offered up to $10,000 in additional mold coverage for a covered peril. Both provisions stated that the coverage does not increase the cap on the underlying damage. The trial court treated the mold benefit as a second pot of money; the three-judge panel disagreed, holding the policy wording was clear — the mold provision is an alternative path to the same $10,000 cap, not a stackable second limit — and remanded to the lower court. The ruling is not yet final while the rehearing window remains open.
  • Why it matters: Florida property-insurance endorsements are read as written, caps included. For any business that files or funds water-damage claims — and AC-system discharge is the fact pattern here — the endorsement's anti-stacking language decides the recovery before anyone argues about the damage itself.
  • Effect on Sharma: Directly adjacent to Sharma's work. HVAC condensate and AC discharge claims are a recurring source of water-damage work; when a homeowner customer expects a mold or water-damage endorsement to fund a larger repair scope, the policy cap — not the damage estimate — may control what the insurer pays. Quote the repair on its merits, get the customer's authorisation for any amount above confirmed coverage in writing, and do not let a job's scope be set by an assumed stack of endorsements.

  1. Construction and contractor law

4.1 Sixth DCA: fraud and FDUTPA claims against a builder's owners belong in arbitration under the construction contract — Zimmerman v. Diez-Arguelles, Wed Nov 25, 2026 (late-located; reported in full Thu Nov 26)

  • What happened: On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Florida Sixth District Court of Appeal (panel including Chief Judge Traver and Judge Brownlee) reversed an Orange County amended final judgment of $126,624.63 against Susan and Tom Zimmerman, the owners of Madison Construction, and remanded with directions to enforce the construction contract's arbitration provision. In 2020, Carlos R. Diez-Arguelles and Maria Tejedor engaged Madison to design and build a $2.775 million waterfront custom home in Tampa, paying a design fee credited toward construction and a deposit totalling $257,500; the project collapsed before construction began. The couple sued, alleging the Zimmermans misrepresented their professional qualifications — including claims they were licensed contractors — and failed to disclose that Madison's licensed contractor, Garland Knight, would not personally supervise the project and that its in-house design professional was not a licensed architect. The trial court found the Zimmermans liable for fraudulent misrepresentation and FDUTPA violations and entered the damages judgment. The Sixth DCA held the trial court erred in refusing to enforce the arbitration clause: the clause was broad enough to cover disputes over the construction contract, the parties' financial dealings and representations made during the relationship; the fraud and FDUTPA claims had a significant contractual nexus to the contract; and under § 23.5, which expressly extends the arbitration clause to the contractor's agents, the Zimmermans — who did not sign in their personal capacities — could enforce it as intended third-party beneficiaries because they acted as Madison's direct agents. The court did not hold the Zimmermans free of liability on the merits; it held the claims must be decided by the arbitrator. The trial court's rulings for all three defendants on the separate theft, statutory civil-theft and conversion claims, and its finding that Knight was not liable on the fraud/FDUTPA claims, were left unchanged.
  • Why it matters: This is the current Florida rule on the two questions every contractor dispute raises: (i) a broadly written arbitration clause reaches the customer's fraud and FDUTPA claims, not just breach-of-contract claims, when they arise from the contractual relationship; and (ii) suing the owners personally does not route around the clause where the contract extends it to agents — personal-capacity tort claims against the people who ran the job still go to the arbitrator. The flip side is equally important: the clause only protects you if your contract has one, written this broadly, with the agent extension spelled out.
  • Effect on Sharma: The most directly applicable item in this edition. Sharma's builder and homeowner contracts should be checked against this template: (i) an arbitration clause covering disputes "arising from or relating to" the contract, the dealings and representations — not just performance; (ii) an express extension to the company's agents, officers and employees, so a suit naming Sanjay or a project manager personally still lands in arbitration; and (iii) consistency between the proposal, the signed contract and any change orders, because the arbitrator will read the clause the parties actually signed. This is a contract-review item for Harvey and Sharma's counsel, not a change to make unilaterally mid-dispute.
