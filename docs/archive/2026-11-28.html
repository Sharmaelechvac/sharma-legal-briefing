1.1 Philadelphia jury awards $264 million over a roofer's fatal fall — Zelinger v. North Point Exteriors LLC, verdict Mon Nov 23, 2026 [widen] (reported Nov 24–27; not previously covered)

  • What happened: On Monday, November 23, 2026, a Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas jury returned a $264 million verdict in Zelinger v. North Point Exteriors LLC, No. 231202369, after a six-day trial before Judge Caroline Turner: $9 million in compensatory (survival and wrongful-death) damages plus $255 million in punitive damages against SRS Distribution Inc. alone, the Texas-based roofing supplier (doing business as Marvic Supply Co.) that Home Depot acquired in 2024. Jose Sicajau Jacinto, 29, fell two stories from an unguarded second-story roof edge onto concrete on July 20, 2022, while unloading crane-lifted plywood at a residential renovation in Southampton, Bucks County, and died on August 1, 2022. The amended complaint alleged no fall protection was installed, contrary to OSHA regulations, and no personal fall-arrest system was provided; as to SRS/Marvic, it alleged the company's crane placed the load in dangerous proximity to the roof edge knowing the roof crew had no fall protection and no guardrails. The jury apportioned fault 65% to SRS, 30% to North Point Exteriors (the general contractor, which had settled before trial and will not pay its share) and 5% to Sicajau, and found SRS acted "outrageously," the Pennsylvania threshold for punitive damages. The Philadelphia Inquirer reported court records show SRS declined to settle for $68.5 million and carries an insurance policy exceeding $200 million. The verdict is a jury verdict, not a final judgment; post-trial motions and an appeal are expected, with SRS having flagged a timeliness objection to the amended complaint.
  • Why it matters: The largest Philadelphia Common Pleas jury verdict of the year so far, and a supplier — not the decedent's employer or even the GC — absorbed the punitive award because its own equipment operation put material at an unprotected edge. Punitive exposure turned on knowledge: delivering to a roof the supplier could see had no fall protection.
  • Effect on Sharma: Direct and practical. Sharma crews work at height and take deliveries on roofs and elevated decks. Three transferable points: (i) fall protection is not only the employer's problem — any party whose work interacts with an unprotected edge can inherit liability; (ii) a documented refusal to work, or to deliver, until protection is in place is the cheapest insurance there is; (iii) settlement posture matters — SRS turned down $68.5 million and took a $264 million verdict. Pennsylvania law, not Florida, but the fact pattern is trade-identical.

2.1 Florida Supreme Court: culpable negligence does not require proof the conduct was likely to kill or seriously injure — A.C. v. State, No. SC2025-0612, Wed Nov 25, 2026 [widen] (not previously covered)

  • What happened: In a unanimous per curiam opinion dated Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Supreme Court of Florida held that the "culpable negligence" element of § 784.05(1), Florida Statutes — exposing another person to personal injury through culpable negligence, a second-degree misdemeanor — does not require proof that the defendant's conduct was likely to cause death or great bodily harm. The line between ordinary and culpable negligence is "driven primarily by the defendant's culpable state of mind" — reckless indifference or grossly careless disregard for the safety of others — reading the statute in light of State v. Greene, 348 So. 2d 3 (Fla. 1977); the likelihood and severity of harm remain relevant to state of mind but are not a separate element. The case itself was a school prank: A.C., a 14-year-old Miami-Dade eighth-grader, smeared clear deodorant gel on a classroom floor during a December 2022 class change; his teacher slipped, fell on her knee and was hurt for months. The Court approved the Third District's decision (A.C. v. State, 409 So. 3d 170 (Fla. 3d DCA 2025)) and disapproved the First District's Kish v. State, 145 So. 3d 225 (Fla. 1st DCA 2014), and the Second District's J.C. v. State, 233 So. 3d 519 (Fla. 2d DCA 2018), which had required the death/great-bodily-harm showing. The opinion carries the standard notice that it is not final until the time to file a rehearing motion expires and any motion filed is determined.
  • Why it matters: Statewide, prosecutors in exposure cases no longer carry the extra proof burden the First and Second Districts imposed — the defence that "nobody was likely to be killed or badly hurt" is gone; the fight is now over reckless disregard.
  • Effect on Sharma: Indirect but real for a field employer. Florida criminal-negligence exposure for reckless workplace conduct — knowingly sending crews into an unsafe condition, for example — no longer requires proof the conduct was likely to kill or seriously injure anyone. Supervision records, safety documentation and a paper trail showing hazards were corrected are what separate an accident from "grossly careless disregard." Compare §1.1: the civil side of the same coin.

3.1 A Florida federal judge refuses to send an overtime case to arbitration because the employer cannot prove the worker ever got the agreement — Diaz v. Topgolf (S.D. Fla.), order filed Wed Nov 25, 2026; reported Fri Nov 27 [widen]

  • What happened: U.S. District Judge Beth Bloom (S.D. Fla.) denied Topgolf's motion to compel arbitration of former office manager Jennifer Diaz's overtime claims, finding no evidence Diaz received the dispute-resolution agreement Topgolf said it sent her by email and through the Workday HR platform. Per the reporting, the agreement was uploaded to Workday while Diaz was on a suspension she alleges followed a complaint, when she had no access to the platform. Her overtime claims therefore proceed in federal court rather than private arbitration. Docket reporting (Law360) dates the order Wednesday, November 25, 2026; Bloomberg Law reported the ruling on Friday, November 27. The ruling decides only the forum question — no wage amounts or class size are reported.
  • Why it matters: The arbitration clause failed on delivery proof, not on its terms. An upload to a portal the employee cannot access is not receipt.
  • Effect on Sharma: The most directly usable employment item of the cycle. If Sharma's hiring paperwork carries an arbitration or dispute-resolution agreement, keep a signed acknowledgment (or an e-sign audit trail showing the employee actually opened it) for every hire — and never rely on a document "sent" while the employee was suspended, on leave or locked out of the system. 4.1 Eleventh Circuit: affiliation alone can debar a whole company family from federally funded work — Figg Group, Inc. v. Federal Highway Administration, No. 26-11283, Wed Nov 25, 2026 [widen] (not previously covered)

  • What happened: On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit (per curiam; Judges Jill Pryor, Branch and Grant) affirmed summary judgment for the Federal Highway Administration, upholding FHWA's debarment of Linda Figg, Figg Group, Inc., Figg Bridge Inspection, Inc., Figg Bridge Builders LLC, Figg Bridge Builders, Inc. and affiliates — the group tied to the Florida International University pedestrian-bridge collapse that killed six people. The plaintiffs were debarred from federally funded projects because of their affiliation with a previously debarred company, even though they engaged in no independent wrongdoing. Reviewing de novo under the Administrative Procedure Act, the court held the Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act and the Nonprocurement Common Rule — specifically 2 C.F.R. § 180.625 — expressly authorize agencies to include affiliates in debarment actions, that affiliation-based debarment is remedial rather than punitive, and that the agency's action was rational and reasonably explained. Appeal from the Northern District of Florida, No. 4:24-cv-00129-AW-MAF.
  • Why it matters: Debarment contagion is now confirmed law in the Eleventh Circuit: one entity's exclusion can lawfully spread across a corporate family on affiliation alone.
  • Effect on Sharma: Real, if remote. Sharma does private builder work, not federal work, so no direct exposure today — but the principle generalises to prequalification and responsibility reviews everywhere: corporate structure is not a shield, and a compliance failure in one entity can be priced against its affiliates. Keep each entity's licensing and compliance record clean and contained.
