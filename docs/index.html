1.1 Judge blocks the FCC's early licence review of Disney's ABC stations as likely First Amendment retaliation — Wed Nov 25, 2026

  • What happened: On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, U.S. District Judge Loren L. AliKhan (District of Columbia) granted The Walt Disney Company and ABC a preliminary injunction barring the Federal Communications Commission from continuing its early review of the licences of eight ABC-owned television stations, and denied the FCC's motion to dismiss the lawsuit. The FCC, chaired by Brendan Carr, had ordered the early renewal review on April 28, 2026 — a day after the President publicly criticised ABC's Jimmy Kimmel — even though the licences were not due for renewal until October 2028 at the earliest. Judge AliKhan wrote, "This is not a close case," found that Carr "has taken President Trump's rhetoric as his marching orders," and held ABC "likely to succeed" on its First Amendment retaliation claim: "No one doubts that the Commission may enforce the law in the manner it sees fit. But in doing so, Defendants may not retaliate against or punish Plaintiffs and other broadcast license holders for disfavored speech." The ruling does not stop separate FCC investigations into Disney's employment practices or The View, and the judge declined ABC's request for a broader injunction against future threats. An FCC spokesperson said Wednesday the portion granting the injunction "is wrong on the facts and wrong on the law."
  • Why it matters: A clean, current statement of the anti-retaliation rule for every regulated business: an agency may enforce vigorously, but the enforcement timing and targeting cannot be punishment for speech the government dislikes — and the regulator's own public statements will be the evidence. Expect an appeal to the D.C. Circuit.
  • Effect on Sharma: No direct effect — Sharma holds no broadcast licence. The transferable lesson is documentary: if a licensing body (DBPR, a local board, a permitting office) ever opens an unusual, out-of-cycle proceeding against Sharma shortly after a dispute, the sequence and the officials' own statements are exactly what a court will read. Keep the paper trail, in writing, from day one.

  • Why it matters: Read together with §1.1, the two same-day rulings are a useful corrective to any "home-team judge" assumption: the same court blocked one administration action on a strong retaliation record and refused to block another where the movant's merits showing fell short. Preliminary-injunction outcomes turn on the likelihood-of-success record, not the politics of the caption.
  • Effect on Sharma: None directly. The practical point for any business that might ever seek or face emergency relief: a TRO is a pause, not a win — the preliminary-injunction stage is where the merits record is actually tested.

3.1 Data privacy in bankruptcy: 42-plus state AGs fight the sale of 21 million users' private chats — objection releases Mon–Tue Nov 23–24 [72-hr widen]; bidder-selection development reported Wed Nov 25

  • What happened: Character Technologies, Inc. (Character.AI) filed Chapter 11 in Delaware on October 12, 2026 (Case No. 26-11603). In releases dated Monday, November 23, 2026 (Minnesota) and Tuesday, November 24, 2026 (California, Connecticut, and others), a bipartisan coalition of 42-plus attorneys general — Florida signed the related earlier objection to the Disney licence sale — asked the bankruptcy court to reject the sale of substantially all of Character.AI's assets, including the platform and the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts (more than 2.1 million registered to minors), with IP addresses and metadata, at an auction set for December 1, 2026. The states argue the sale terms impose no obligations on the buyer to follow Character.AI's privacy policy (which told users "we do not sell personal information for monetary consideration"), to honour deletion or opt-out requests, or to limit use of the data — and that bankruptcy does not free a company from consumer-protection and privacy law. The only disclosed bidder, Smirks & Pangs Acquisition LLC, was formed in Delaware on November 13, 2026, one day before it was selected as the base bidder. Evening reporting on Wednesday, November 25, 2026 states the bankruptcy judge approved that bidder selection on Tuesday and that California AG Bonta called the proposed sale "appalling" in a Tuesday statement; a separate hearing on Disney's $20 million nonexclusive licence bid is set for December 3–4, 2026.
  • Why it matters: This is the leading current test of whether customer data collected under a "we do not sell your information" promise can be sold as a bankruptcy asset over the objection of most of the country's attorneys general. The states' theory — the promise binds the data, not just the company — is the one any buyer of a business's customer list will now have to price in.
  • Effect on Sharma: Sharma's customer data is smaller but the rule is the same shape: the CRM's customer list, service histories, and any text-message records are business assets whose transfer (sale of the business, a book-of-business deal, even a platform migration) is governed by what customers were told when the data was collected. Do not promise "we never share your information" in marketing copy unless the business means it in a sale too — and if Sharma ever buys another contractor's customer list, ask what those customers were promised.
